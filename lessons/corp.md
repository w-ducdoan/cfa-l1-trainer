# v3m1
take: Sole proprietorships and general partnerships are simple but carry unlimited liability; limited companies add a separate legal identity, limited liability, owner-manager separation and easier financing, at the cost of possible double taxation; public companies trade liquid shares with heavy disclosure, private ones don't.
mins: 25

## Organizational forms
| | Sole proprietorship | General partnership | Limited partnership | Limited company (corporation) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal identity | Same as owner | Same as owners | GP and LPs | Separate legal entity |
| Liability | Unlimited | Unlimited, joint | GP unlimited; LPs limited to investment | Limited to investment |
| Owner–manager | Same person | Partners manage | GP manages; LPs passive | Separated: shareholders own, managers run |
| Taxation | Personal (pass-through) | Pass-through | Pass-through | Corporate tax, then tax on dividends (double), unless a pass-through form applies |
| Financing | Owner's resources, loans | Partners' capital | Can raise from many LPs | Easiest: shares and bonds to many investors |
| Life | Ends with owner | Ends if a partner leaves (unless agreed) | Fixed or agreed | Perpetual |

Limited partnerships are common in private equity and real estate funds: the general partner runs the fund; limited partners supply most of the money.

**Double taxation** example: corporate profit 100, corporate tax 25% → 75 after tax. Paid as a dividend taxed at 20% → shareholder keeps 60. Effective tax rate = **40%**.

## Public vs. private
| | Public | Private |
|---|---|---|
| Shares | Listed on an exchange; liquid; prices visible | No public market; illiquid; negotiated prices |
| Issuance | IPO, then follow-on offerings to the public | Private placements to selected investors |
| Disclosure | Extensive, regular, audited filings | Limited |
| Ownership | Often dispersed | Concentrated: founders, families, PE firms |

**Going public**: IPO, direct listing, or merger with a SPAC. **Going private**: a buyout (often leveraged, LBO, or by management, MBO) by investors who take the company off the exchange.

Owners vary: retail investors, institutions (pension funds, insurers, asset managers), founders and families, private equity, governments and state-owned enterprises, and other companies (cross-holdings).

# v3m2
take: Lenders hold a fixed, prior claim with capped upside; shareholders hold a residual claim with unlimited upside, so they like risk more than lenders do; many stakeholders have interests in the firm, and investors weigh material ESG factors.
mins: 20

## Debt vs. equity
| | Debt | Equity |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | Fixed interest and principal; priority | Residual: what's left |
| Maturity | Finite | Permanent |
| Upside | Capped at promised payments | Unlimited |
| Downside | Loss if default, with recovery | Can lose everything |
| Control | Covenants, no votes | Votes |
| Cost to issuer | Lower (priority, tax-deductible interest) | Higher |

Example: debt of $100. If the firm is worth $80, lenders get $80 and shareholders $0. If it's worth $150, lenders get $100 and shareholders $50. Equity behaves like a **call option** on the firm's assets.

## Conflicts between them
Shareholders gain more from upside and are protected on the downside by limited liability, so they may favor **riskier projects**, more **debt**, and large **dividends or buybacks** that move value away from lenders. Lenders respond with covenants, collateral and higher rates.

## Stakeholders
| Group | Main interest |
|---|---|
| Shareholders | Value of their shares; dividends |
| Creditors | Being repaid; stable cash flow |
| Board of directors | Oversight; protecting shareholders (and other stakeholders) |
| Managers | Pay, job security, reputation |
| Employees | Wages, safety, job security, development |
| Customers | Quality, price, safety, data protection |
| Suppliers | Being paid; long-term relationship |
| Governments | Taxes, employment, compliance, public welfare |

**Shareholder theory**: the company's goal is to maximize shareholder value. **Stakeholder theory**: balance the interests of all stakeholders.

## ESG
- **Environmental**: emissions and climate risk, pollution, resource use, waste.
- **Social**: labor practices, human rights in supply chains, product safety, data privacy, community relations.
- **Governance**: board independence and skills, executive pay, shareholder rights, accounting quality, ethics.

Investors focus on factors that are **material**: likely to affect cash flows, risk or reputation for that industry. ESG factors can be risks (fines, stranded assets, boycotts) or opportunities (new markets, efficiency).

# v3m3
take: Agents (managers, directors, controlling holders) can act against principals (shareholders, minority holders, creditors); governance mechanisms such as disclosure, voting, board structure, pay design, covenants and regulation limit the damage; good governance lowers operational, legal and financial risk.
mins: 25

## Principal-agent conflicts
A **principal** hires an **agent** to act in the principal's interest; their interests can diverge.

| Relationship | Typical conflicts |
|---|---|
| Shareholders vs. managers and directors | Too little effort; **empire building**; entrenchment (resisting takeovers); too much or too little risk; perks; pay unrelated to performance; information asymmetry |
| Controlling vs. minority shareholders | Related-party transactions; dual-class control; decisions that favor the controller |
| Shareholders vs. creditors | Riskier projects, more debt, large payouts |
| Others | Employees, customers, suppliers and governments each want different things |

## Governance mechanisms
- **Reporting and transparency**: audited financial statements, disclosure of ownership, pay and risks.
- **Shareholders**: annual and extraordinary general meetings; **ordinary resolutions** (simple majority) and **special resolutions** (supermajority, e.g., mergers); proxy voting; **say on pay**; activism; derivative lawsuits; the threat of takeover (the market for corporate control).
- **Creditors**: bond indentures and covenants, collateral, creditor committees in distress.
- **Board and management**: independent directors; board committees (**audit**, **compensation**, **nomination**, risk, governance); separation of chair and CEO; pay with long-term incentives and clawbacks.
- **Employees**: labor laws, contracts, employee share plans, works councils.
- **Customers and suppliers**: contracts and the ability to walk away.
- **Governments**: laws, regulations, corporate governance codes.

## Risks and benefits
| Area | Poor governance risk | Good governance benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Operational | Weak control, poor decisions | Better decisions and efficiency |
| Legal, regulatory, reputational | Fines, lawsuits, scandals | Compliance, trust |
| Financial | Higher default risk, higher cost of capital, value destruction | Lower cost of capital, better access to funding |

Analysts' questions: Who owns and controls the company, and how do votes work? Are the board's skills right and is it independent? Is pay tied to the right results? Who are the big investors? How strong are shareholder rights versus peers?

take: The cash conversion cycle (DOH + DSO − DPO) shows how long cash is tied up; liquidity comes from primary sources (cash, operations, short-term funding) and secondary ones (renegotiating, selling assets, bankruptcy protection); and working capital can be managed conservatively or aggressively.
mins: 25

## Cash conversion cycle
The **cash conversion cycle (CCC)** is the number of days between paying suppliers and collecting cash from customers: how long a company's money is tied up in running the business.

```
CCC = Days of inventory on hand + Days sales outstanding − Days payables outstanding
```
~ Days of inventory on hand (DOH) :: average days inventory sits before being sold
~ Days sales outstanding (DSO) :: average days to collect from customers
~ Days payables outstanding (DPO) :: average days the company takes to pay its suppliers
| Company | DOH | DSO | DPO | CCC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (manufacturer) | 60 | 45 | 40 | **65 days** |
| B (grocer) | 30 | 20 | 55 | **−5 days** |

A short (even negative) CCC means suppliers are funding the business; a long one ties up cash and needs financing. Compare within an industry: CCCs differ a lot across industries.

## Liquidity
liquidity :: the ability to meet short-term obligations as they come due
primary sources :: cash, short-term investments, cash from operations, bank lines and short-term funding
secondary sources :: renegotiating debt, selling assets, filing for bankruptcy protection; they change the company's operations or financial position

**Drags** slow cash coming in: uncollected receivables, obsolete inventory, tight credit. **Pulls** speed cash going out: paying suppliers early, reduced credit limits, lenders calling loans.

Measures: current, quick and cash ratios; the CCC; and the amount of committed undrawn credit.

## Managing working capital
| Approach | Current assets | Funding | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | High (more cash and inventory) | More long-term funding | Lower risk, lower return |
| Aggressive | Low | More short-term funding | Higher return, more liquidity and rollover risk |
| Moderate | In between, matching maturities | Mix | Balance |

Levers: collect receivables faster (credit terms, factoring), hold less inventory (just-in-time), and use supplier credit, while weighing the cost.

**Trade credit** has a cost if you skip an early-payment discount. Terms "2/10 net 30" (2% off if paid in 10 days, otherwise due in 30):
```
Cost = (1 + 2/98)^(365/20) − 1 = 44.6% a year
```
~ 2/98 :: the 2% discount you give up, relative to the 98% you'd have paid early
~ 20 :: the extra days of credit you get by not paying early (30 − 10)
~ 365/20 :: how many 20-day periods fit in a year
Skipping the discount is very expensive financing; pay early unless your funding costs more.

# v3m5
take: Rank projects by NPV (accept if positive; pick the higher NPV when projects conflict); IRR is the break-even rate; ROIC above the cost of capital creates value; use incremental after-tax cash flows, ignore sunk costs, and value the real options embedded in projects.
mins: 35

## Types of capital investments
1. **Going concern**: maintain the business or improve efficiency (replacing equipment).
2. **Regulatory and compliance**: required by law or safety rules; often negative NPV but necessary.
3. **Expansion** of existing business.
4. **Other**: new lines of business, acquisitions, high-risk projects.

## The capital allocation process
Generate ideas → analyze proposals → plan and prioritize → monitor and conduct a **post-audit** (compare actual results to forecasts).

## NPV and IRR
Net present value (NPV) answers: after accounting for the time value of money, does this project create more value than it costs? The internal rate of return (IRR) is the project's own break-even rate of return.

```
NPV = Σ CFt / (1 + r)^t − Initial investment
IRR = the r that makes NPV = 0
```
~ NPV :: net present value
~ CFt :: the cash flow in year t
~ r :: the cost of capital (the return investors require)
~ Initial investment :: the cash spent at the start
~ IRR :: internal rate of return
Rules: accept if **NPV > 0**, or if **IRR > cost of capital**.

Example: invest $1,000; receive $450 a year for 3 years; cost of capital 10%.
- PV = 450 × 2.4869 = 1,119.08. **NPV = +$119.08**: accept.
- IRR ≈ **16.6%** (above 10%).

When **mutually exclusive** projects rank differently on NPV and IRR (because of scale or timing), choose the **higher NPV**: it measures the value added. IRR assumes reinvestment at the IRR and can have multiple answers when cash flows change sign more than once.

## ROIC
Return on invested capital (ROIC) measures how much after-tax operating profit a company earns on the money invested in it; comparing it with the cost of capital shows whether it creates value.

```
ROIC = After-tax operating profit / Average invested capital
```
~ ROIC :: return on invested capital
~ After-tax operating profit :: operating profit (EBIT) × (1 − tax rate)
~ Average invested capital :: average debt plus equity used in the business over the year
Value is created when **ROIC > cost of capital**. It's a company-level, accounting-based check, useful but backward-looking.

## Principles and pitfalls
Principles:
- Base decisions on **incremental, after-tax cash flows**, not accounting income.
- Account for **timing**: earlier cash is worth more.
- Include **opportunity costs** (using land you already own) and **externalities** (cannibalizing existing sales).
- **Ignore sunk costs** (money already spent).
- Don't subtract financing costs from cash flows; they're in the discount rate.

Pitfalls: budgets anchored to last year (inertia), treating internal funds as free, pet projects, too few alternatives, using a single hurdle rate for projects of different risk, chasing EPS or ROE rather than NPV.

## Real options
| Option | Value comes from |
|---|---|
| Timing | Waiting for better information before investing |
| Abandonment | Exiting if results are poor |
| Expansion (growth) | Scaling up if results are good |
| Flexibility | Changing prices or production methods |
| Fundamental | The project's payoff itself depends on an asset price (e.g., a mine and the gold price) |

Project NPV with options = NPV without options + value of options − cost of options.

## The cost of capital
A company's cost of capital is the blended return its lenders and shareholders require. The **weighted average cost of capital (WACC)** weights each source by its share of the company's financing.

```
WACC = wd × rd × (1 − t) + wp × rp + we × re
```
~ wd, wp, we :: weights of debt, preferred stock and common equity (market values, adding to 1)
~ rd :: before-tax cost of debt
~ t :: the marginal tax rate (interest is tax-deductible, so debt costs rd × (1 − t))
~ rp :: cost of preferred stock
~ re :: cost of common equity
Use market-value **target** weights. Interest is tax-deductible, so the cost of debt is after tax.

Example: 60% equity at 11%, 40% debt at 6%, tax 25%.
WACC = 0.6 × 11% + 0.4 × 6% × 0.75 = 6.6% + 1.8% = **8.4%**.

## What drives capital structure
- **Internal**: business model and cash flow stability (stable → more debt capacity), tangible assets (good collateral), growth and life-cycle stage (start-ups use equity; mature firms carry more debt), existing leverage, management's preferences.
- **External**: market conditions and interest rates, industry norms, regulation, tax rules.

## Modigliani–Miller
Assumptions: perfect markets, no taxes or distress costs, everyone borrows at the same rate.

| Proposition | Without taxes | With corporate taxes |
|---|---|---|
| I (value) | V_L = V_U: leverage doesn't change firm value | V_L = V_U + t × D: debt adds the tax shield |
| II (cost of equity) | re = r0 + (r0 − rd) × D/E | re = r0 + (r0 − rd) × (1 − t) × D/E |

r0 = cost of capital of the unlevered firm. As debt rises, equity gets riskier, so re rises. Without taxes, WACC stays constant; with taxes, WACC falls as debt rises.

Example: r0 = 10%, rd = 6%, D/E = 1.
- No taxes: re = 10% + 4% × 1 = **14%**.
- Tax 25%: re = 10% + 4% × 0.75 = **13%**.
- Value with taxes: unlevered value $500, debt $200 → V_L = 500 + 0.25 × 200 = **$550**.

## Financial distress and the optimal structure
Distress costs: **direct** (legal and administrative costs of bankruptcy) and **indirect** (lost customers and suppliers, forgone investment, management distraction). Their expected value rises with leverage.

**Static trade-off theory**:
```
V_L = V_U + t × D − PV(costs of financial distress)
```
~ V_L :: value of the company with debt (levered)
~ V_U :: value of the same company with no debt (unlevered)
~ t × D :: the value of the tax shield from debt (tax rate × debt)
~ PV(costs of financial distress) :: today's value of expected bankruptcy and distress costs
The optimal structure is where the marginal tax benefit equals the marginal expected distress cost; that's also where WACC is lowest. Companies set a **target** capital structure and move toward it over time.

**Pecking order theory**: because managers know more than investors, issuing equity signals overvaluation. Companies prefer internal funds, then debt, then (last) equity. **Agency costs**: debt can discipline managers by reducing free cash flow, but too much creates shareholder-creditor conflicts.

take: A business model answers who the customers are, what the firm offers and why, where it reaches them, and how much it charges; know the pricing models, value proposition and value chain, conventional models and their variations, and how network effects power platforms.

## The core questions
| Question | Element |
|---|---|
| Who? | Target customers and segments |
| What, and why? | Products and services; the **value proposition**: why customers choose it over rivals |
| Where? | Channels: direct, retail, online, distributors |
| How much? | Pricing and revenue model |

The **value chain** is how the firm organizes its activities (sourcing, production, marketing, delivery, service) to create that value; firms decide what to do in-house and what to outsource or partner on.

## Pricing and revenue models
- **Value-based** pricing (what the customer values) vs. **cost-plus** pricing.
- **Price discrimination**: tiered pricing, volume discounts, dynamic pricing (airlines, ride sharing), auctions.
- **Bundling**: products sold together.
- **Razors and blades**: cheap device, profitable consumables.
- **Freemium**: free basic version, paid premium.
- **Subscription** and **recurring revenue**: predictable cash flows.
- **Licensing** and **franchising**: others pay to use the brand or intellectual property.

## Business model types
**Conventional**: manufacturers, distributors and wholesalers, retailers, service providers.

**Variations**: private label (selling others' products under the retailer's brand), value-added resellers, licensing, franchising.

**Innovations**: e-commerce and direct-to-consumer, asset-light models (outsourced production), "as-a-service" offerings, crowdsourcing.

## Network effects and platforms
**Network effects**: a product becomes more valuable as more people use it. **Direct** (more users on a messaging app) or **indirect** (more buyers attract more sellers on a marketplace).

**Platforms** connect two or more groups (buyers and sellers, riders and drivers, users and advertisers) and take a fee. Strong network effects create winner-take-most markets and a moat, but platforms need a critical mass to start and may burn cash early.
