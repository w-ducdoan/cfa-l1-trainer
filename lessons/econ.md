# v2m1
take: Profit is maximized where marginal revenue = marginal cost; break even at price = average total cost, shut down in the short run when price < average variable cost; and know the four market structures, how each prices, and how concentration is measured.
mins: 35

## Profit, breakeven and shutdown
economic profit :: total revenue − all opportunity costs (including a normal return on owners' capital)
accounting profit :: total revenue − explicit (accounting) costs; higher than economic profit
marginal revenue (MR) :: extra revenue from one more unit
marginal cost (MC) :: extra cost of one more unit

- **Profit maximum**: produce where **MR = MC** (and MC isn't falling).
- **Breakeven**: price = **average total cost (ATC)**: zero economic profit.
- **Short-run shutdown**: if price < **average variable cost (AVC)**, stop producing. Between AVC and ATC, keep operating: you cover variable costs and part of fixed costs, losing less than if you shut.
- **Long-run exit**: if price < ATC with no prospect of improvement.

| Price relative to costs | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| P ≥ ATC | Operate (profit) | Stay |
| AVC ≤ P < ATC | Operate (loss, but covering variable costs) | Exit |
| P < AVC | Shut down | Exit |

## Economies of scale
Long-run ATC (all inputs variable) falls with output under **economies of scale** (specialization, bargaining power with suppliers) and rises under **diseconomies** (bureaucracy, coordination problems). The lowest point is the **minimum efficient scale**.

## The four market structures
| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sellers | Many | Many | Few | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated | Unique, no close substitutes |
| Barriers to entry | Very low | Low | High | Very high |
| Pricing power | None (price taker) | Some | Some to significant, interdependent | Significant |
| Non-price competition | None | Advertising, quality, brand | Some | Advertising |
| Long-run economic profit | Zero | Zero | Possible | Possible |

- **Perfect competition**: horizontal demand; P = MR = MC.
- **Monopolistic competition**: downward-sloping demand, P > MR; short-run profits attract entry until long-run economic profit is zero, with **excess capacity** (output below the minimum-ATC level).
- **Oligopoly**: firms react to each other.
  - **Kinked demand**: rivals match price cuts but not increases, so prices are sticky.
  - **Cournot**: each firm sets output assuming rivals' output is fixed; result lies between competition and monopoly.
  - **Nash equilibrium**: no firm can do better by changing its choice alone. Collusion would earn more but is unstable.
  - **Dominant firm (Stackelberg)**: a leader sets price; followers take it.
- **Monopoly**: sets MR = MC, charges the price on the demand curve; may **price discriminate**; often regulated.

## Measuring concentration
```
N-firm concentration ratio = sum of the N largest market shares
HHI = sum of squared market shares (in %)
```
Shares 40%, 30%, 20%, 10%: four-firm ratio = 100%; HHI = 3,000. **Limitations**: they ignore barriers to entry and potential competitors, and depend on how the market is defined. Econometric estimates of demand elasticity are an alternative.

## Phases
| Phase | Output vs. potential | Typical signs |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery | Trough; actual output furthest below potential, starting to rise | Confidence improving, inventories rebuilt, firms add hours before hiring |
| Expansion | Growth above trend; output rises above potential (boom) | Hiring, capex, inflation and rates start rising |
| Slowdown | Output peaks above potential; growth slows | Inflation high, rates high, profits squeezed |
| Contraction | Output below potential and falling | Layoffs, falling capex, inventories cut, inflation eases |

Three ways to define the cycle: **classical** (the level of output), **growth** (output relative to trend) and **growth rate** (the growth rate itself).

**Leads and lags**: firms adjust hours and overtime before hiring or firing; capital spending follows profits and capacity use; inventories swing: the **inventory-to-sales ratio** rises at the start of a downturn (sales fall faster than production is cut) and falls in recovery.

## Credit cycles
Credit availability and pricing expand and contract too, usually longer and larger than business cycles. Easy credit fuels property and asset booms; strong credit peaks often precede **banking crises**. Policymakers watch credit growth to lean against booms.

## Economic indicators
| Type | Turns | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Leading | Before the economy | Average weekly manufacturing hours, initial jobless claims, new orders, building permits, stock prices, the yield curve spread, consumer expectations |
| Coincident | With it | Payroll employment, industrial production, real personal income, manufacturing and trade sales |
| Lagging | After it | Average duration of unemployment, inventory-to-sales ratio, unit labor costs, the prime rate, commercial loans outstanding, consumer credit to income, service-sector inflation |

- **Composite indexes** combine several indicators.
- A **diffusion index** shows how many components are rising: a measure of breadth.
- **Surveys** such as purchasing managers' indexes (above 50 means expansion).
- **Nowcasting** estimates current-quarter GDP from incoming data, often using big data (card spending, mobility).

# v2m3
take: Fiscal policy uses spending and taxes to allocate resources, redistribute income and stabilize the economy; the multiplier is 1/[1 − c(1 − t)]; judge the stance by the structural, not the headline, deficit; and expect recognition, action and impact lags.

## Fiscal vs. monetary policy
**Fiscal policy**: the government's taxing and spending. **Monetary policy**: the central bank's control of money and credit. Both aim at stable growth; fiscal policy also targets the distribution of income and wealth.

Roles of fiscal policy: **allocation** of resources (public goods), **redistribution** (taxes and transfers), **stabilization** of the cycle.

## Does debt matter?
| Concerns | Counterarguments |
|---|---|
| High debt may need higher future taxes, discouraging work and investment | Much debt is owed to domestic citizens |
| Investors may lose confidence; rates rise | Borrowing for productive investment can pay for itself |
| **Crowding out**: government borrowing raises rates and displaces private investment | **Ricardian equivalence**: people save more anticipating future taxes, offsetting the stimulus |
| Printing money to pay it risks inflation | Deficits can be useful when the economy has spare capacity |

## Tools
- **Spending**: transfer payments (pensions, unemployment benefits), current spending (wages, goods and services), capital spending (infrastructure).
- **Revenue**: direct taxes (income, wealth, corporate) and indirect taxes (sales, VAT, excise).
- Good tax design: **simple, efficient** (little distortion), **fair** (horizontal and vertical equity), **sufficient** revenue.
- Indirect taxes can be changed quickly; capital spending takes time to plan and spend.

## The multiplier
```
Fiscal multiplier = 1 / [1 − c(1 − t)]      c = marginal propensity to consume, t = tax rate
```
Example: c = 0.8, t = 25%. c(1 − t) = 0.6. Multiplier = 1/0.4 = **2.5**: $10bn of extra spending raises GDP by about $25bn.

**Balanced budget multiplier**: raising spending and taxes by the same amount is still expansionary (spending is fully injected; part of the tax would have been saved).

## Stance and difficulties
- **Automatic stabilizers** (progressive taxes, unemployment benefits) cushion the cycle without new decisions.
- **Discretionary** policy is a deliberate change.
- The **structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit** is the deficit at full employment; a rising structural deficit signals **expansionary** policy. A headline deficit can rise just because of recession.
- **Lags**: recognition (spotting the problem), action (passing laws), impact (effects on the economy).
- Other problems: misjudging the economy, conflicting goals, crowding out, and supply-side limits when the economy is near full capacity.

# v2m4
take: Central banks target price stability using open market operations, the policy rate and reserve requirements; policy is tight when the policy rate is above neutral (trend real growth + inflation target); effectiveness needs independence, credibility and transparency; and fiscal and monetary mixes have predictable effects.
mins: 30

## Roles and objectives
Central banks: supply currency, act as banker to the government and to banks, serve as **lender of last resort**, run the payments system, conduct monetary policy and often supervise banks. The main objective is **price stability**; some (like the US Fed) also target maximum employment, and many consider financial stability.

## Tools
- **Open market operations**: buying government securities adds reserves (expansionary); selling drains them.
- **Policy rate**: the rate at which the central bank lends to banks (or the target for the interbank rate).
- **Reserve requirements**: higher requirements reduce lending; rarely changed now.
- **Quantitative easing**: large-scale asset purchases when the policy rate is near zero.

**Transmission**: policy rate → market rates, asset prices, expectations and the exchange rate → spending, investment and net exports → output and inflation. A rate cut lowers borrowing costs, lifts asset prices, and tends to weaken the currency, boosting exports.

## Neutral rate and stance
```
Neutral rate = Trend real growth + Inflation target
```
Example: trend growth 2%, inflation target 2% → neutral 4%. A policy rate of 5% is **contractionary**; 3% is **expansionary**.

## Effective central banks
- **Independence**: operational (sets rates to meet a target) and sometimes target independence (sets the target itself).
- **Credibility**: people believe it will hit its target, so expectations stay anchored.
- **Transparency**: clear communication, published forecasts, minutes.

**Inflation targeting**: a public target (often 2%), a forecast-driven approach. **Exchange rate targeting**: peg to another currency; the country effectively imports the anchor country's monetary policy and loses independent control (interest rates must follow the anchor).

## Limitations
- Long and variable lags.
- **Liquidity trap**: at very low rates, extra money is hoarded, not spent.
- **Deflation**: nominal rates can't go far below zero, so real rates stay too high.
- Bond markets may not cooperate: long rates can rise if investors doubt the policy.
- Supply shocks raise inflation and cut output at the same time, a hard trade-off.

## Fiscal and monetary policy together
| Fiscal | Monetary | Likely result |
|---|---|---|
| Easy | Easy | Strongly expansionary; aggregate demand up sharply |
| Tight | Tight | Contractionary |
| Easy | Tight | Higher interest rates; public sector grows relative to private |
| Tight | Easy | Lower interest rates; private sector grows relative to public |

# v2m5
take: Geopolitics is how countries cooperate or compete, shaped by national interest, resources and institutions; combine cooperation with globalization to get four archetypes; the IMF, World Bank and WTO support the system; and judge geopolitical risk by type, likelihood, speed and impact.
mins: 25

## Actors and cooperation
**State actors** (governments) and **non-state actors** (companies, NGOs, individuals) pursue their interests. Countries cooperate for military, economic or cultural reasons, influenced by:
- **Resource endowment**: what they have and need.
- **Standardization**: shared rules and standards that make cooperation cheaper.
- **Soft power** and culture.
- A **hierarchy of national interests**: survival first, then economic and other goals.
- The **power of the decision maker** and the role of institutions.

## Globalization vs. nationalism
**Globalization**: open trade, capital flows, information and cultural exchange. Gains: higher productivity, access to markets and resources, lower costs, shared knowledge. Costs: unequal benefits, job losses in some sectors, dependence on supply chains, contagion. **Nationalism** puts a country's interests ahead of others and limits cooperation.

## Archetypes
| | Nationalism | Globalization |
|---|---|---|
| Cooperative | **Bilateralism**: deals country by country | **Multilateralism**: many-country rules and institutions |
| Non-cooperative | **Autarky**: self-sufficiency, state-led | **Hegemony**: open but uses dominance to shape others |

## International organizations
- **IMF**: stability of the international monetary system; surveillance and emergency lending to countries in balance-of-payments trouble.
- **World Bank Group**: fights poverty in developing countries; long-term development finance and technical help.
- **WTO**: rules for global trade; negotiates agreements and settles trade disputes.

## Tools of geopolitics
- **National security**: armed conflict, alliances, espionage.
- **Economic**: cooperative (free trade agreements) or non-cooperative (tariffs, export controls, nationalization).
- **Financial**: currency reserve status and payment systems, sanctions, restrictions on foreign investment.

## Geopolitical risk and investing
| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Event risk | Known timing | Elections, treaty deadlines |
| Exogenous risk | Sudden and unanticipated | Invasions, uprisings, natural disasters |
| Thematic risk | Known, evolving over time | Climate change, cyber threats, pandemics |

Assess **likelihood**, **velocity** (how fast it hits: high-velocity risks hit markets quickly; low-velocity ones erode value slowly) and **size and nature of impact** (on one company, a sector or the whole market). Use **scenario analysis** and track **signposts** (signals that a scenario is becoming more or less likely). Responses: diversify, hedge, adjust exposures, or simply be aware.

take: Trade lets countries specialize by comparative advantage; tariffs, quotas and export subsidies help domestic producers at consumers' expense and create deadweight losses; trading blocs deepen from free trade areas to economic and monetary unions.

## Benefits and costs
**Comparative advantage**: a country should produce what it makes at a lower **opportunity cost** than others, even if it has no absolute advantage in anything. Gains: more consumption, access to cheaper goods, economies of scale, more competition and innovation. Costs: job losses and adjustment in industries that face imports; income inequality; dependence on others.

## Trade restrictions
| Tool | How it works | Who gains | Who loses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tariff** | Tax on imports | Domestic producers; government (revenue) | Consumers; deadweight loss for the economy |
| **Import quota** | Limit on import quantity | Domestic producers; whoever holds the licenses (quota rents) | Consumers; deadweight loss |
| **Voluntary export restraint** | Exporting country limits its exports | Foreign exporters capture the rents | Importing country's consumers |
| **Export subsidy** | Government pays exporters | Domestic exporters | Domestic consumers (higher domestic price), taxpayers; may trigger countervailing duties |

For a small country, a tariff or quota always reduces national welfare. A **large** country can sometimes gain by pushing down world prices (terms-of-trade effect).

## Trading blocs
| Level | Free trade inside | Common external trade policy | Free movement of labor and capital | Common economic policies and institutions | Common currency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free trade area | Yes | | | | |
| Customs union | Yes | Yes | | | |
| Common market | Yes | Yes | Yes | | |
| Economic union | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | |
| Monetary union | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |

Benefits: larger markets, more competition, economies of scale, **trade creation** (cheaper imports from members replace domestic production). Risks: **trade diversion** (cheaper non-member imports replaced by costlier member ones), loss of sovereignty, and in a monetary union, no independent monetary policy.

# v2m7
take: The FX market is the world's largest; quotes are price currency per unit of base currency; real exchange rates adjust for price levels; regimes range from no separate currency to a free float; and a current account deficit is mirrored by a capital account surplus.
mins: 25

## The market
Participants: **sell-side** dealers (banks), and the **buy-side**: corporations, real money managers, leveraged funds, retail, governments, central banks and sovereign wealth funds. Products: spot, forwards, FX swaps, futures and options. Uses: trade settlement, hedging, investment and speculation.

## Quotes
A quote like **USD/EUR = 1.1000** means 1.1000 US dollars per 1 euro: EUR is the **base** currency, USD the **price** currency. (Market conventions vary; always check which is which.)

If USD/EUR rises from 1.1000 to 1.2100:
- The **euro appreciated** 10% against the dollar: 1.21/1.10 − 1.
- The **dollar depreciated** 9.09% against the euro: (1/1.21)/(1/1.10) − 1 = 1.10/1.21 − 1.

Appreciation of one is not the same percentage as depreciation of the other.

## Real exchange rates
The real exchange rate adjusts the nominal rate for price levels: it measures **relative purchasing power**.
```
Real rate (domestic per foreign) = Nominal rate × (Foreign price level / Domestic price level)
```
If foreign prices rise faster than domestic ones with the nominal rate unchanged, foreign goods become relatively more expensive: the real value of the foreign currency rises.

## Exchange rate regimes
From most fixed to most flexible:
1. **No separate legal tender** (dollarization) or a **monetary union**.
2. **Currency board**: fixed rate, fully backed by foreign reserves.
3. **Conventional fixed peg** (narrow band).
4. **Target zone** (wider band).
5. **Crawling peg** and crawling band (adjusted periodically).
6. **Managed float**.
7. **Independent float**.

No regime offers all three of fixed rates, free capital flows and independent monetary policy at once.

## Trade and capital flows
The balance of payments links them: a **current account deficit** (importing more than exporting) must be financed by a **capital/financial account surplus** (net inflows of investment). Exchange rate changes affect the trade balance over time; a weaker currency makes exports cheaper and imports dearer.

## Capital restrictions
Governments may restrict flows to: reduce volatile short-term inflows, prevent **capital flight** in a crisis, keep the currency competitive, protect strategic industries, or for national security. Restrictions lower efficiency and can deter investment.

take: Get cross-rates by chaining quotes so the middle currency cancels; forwards follow covered interest parity, F = S × (1 + i_price × t)/(1 + i_base × t), so the higher-rate currency trades at a forward discount; forward points are F − S in pips.
mins: 25

## Cross-rates
When a pair isn't quoted directly, combine two quotes so the shared currency cancels.

Example: USD/EUR = 1.1000 and JPY/USD = 150.00.
```
JPY/EUR = JPY/USD × USD/EUR = 150.00 × 1.1000 = 165.00
```
If the common currency doesn't cancel, invert one quote first (USD/JPY = 1/150). Quoted cross-rates that don't match the implied rate create a (rare, brief) arbitrage opportunity.

## Forward rates and covered interest parity
Investing in either currency and hedging with a forward must give the same result; otherwise there's riskless arbitrage.
```
F (price/base) = S × (1 + i_price × Days/360) / (1 + i_base × Days/360)
```
Example: spot USD/EUR 1.1000; 1-year USD rate 5%, EUR rate 3%.
F = 1.1000 × 1.05/1.03 = **1.1214**.

- Forward points = (F − S) × 10,000 = +213.6 points (most pairs are quoted to 4 decimals; yen pairs to 2).
- As a percentage: 1.1214/1.1000 − 1 = **+1.94%**.

## Premium or discount
The **base currency** trades at a **forward premium** when F > S, and a **discount** when F < S. The currency with the **higher interest rate trades at a forward discount** (so you can't earn the rate gap risk-free). Above, EUR (base, lower rate) is at a premium; USD is at a discount.

To get the outright forward from points: F = S + points/10,000. Example: spot 1.3000, 3-month points −45.0 → F = 1.2955.
