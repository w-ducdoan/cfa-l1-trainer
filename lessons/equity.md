# v5m1
take: Common shares are the residual claim with votes; preferred shares sit above common with fixed, usually non-voting dividends; public equity is liquid and disclosed, private equity is concentrated, illiquid and spans venture capital to buyouts.
mins: 20

## What equity gives you
Equity is ownership. Its rights:
- **Economic**: dividends, a share of the proceeds in liquidation (after creditors), and sometimes conversion.
- **Legal and voting**: electing directors and voting on major decisions.
- **Limited liability**: you can lose what you invested, nothing more.

| | Common shares | Preferred (preference) shares |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | Residual: last in line | Senior to common, junior to debt |
| Dividends | Discretionary, can grow | Fixed, paid before common; non-discretionary in practice |
| Voting | Yes | Usually no |
| Upside | Unlimited | Limited (unless convertible or participating) |

Preferred variations:
cumulative :: missed dividends accrue and must be paid before any common dividend
non-cumulative :: missed dividends are lost
participating :: extra dividends if profits exceed a level, plus a share of liquidation surplus
convertible :: can switch into common shares; gives upside
callable :: the issuer can buy back at a set price, capping upside (lowers value)
putable :: the holder can sell back at a set price (raises value)

Voting methods: **statutory** (one vote per share per director) vs. **cumulative** (votes can be pooled on one candidate, helping minority holders get a board seat).

## Public vs. private equity
| | Publicly listed | Private |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Exchange or OTC, meeting listing rules | Negotiated, no public market |
| Disclosure | Extensive, regular | Limited; costs less, avoids scrutiny |
| Liquidity | High | Low; long holding periods |
| Ownership | Dispersed | Concentrated: controlling or large minority stakes |
| Pricing | Continuous | Periodic valuations |

Private equity across the life cycle:
- **Venture capital**: seed and early stage, start-ups, high risk.
- **Growth equity**: minority stakes in expanding companies.
- **Buyouts**: control of mature companies, often with heavy debt (**LBO**) or by management (**MBO**).
- **PIPE**: private investment in a public company, usually when it needs capital fast.

# v5m2
take: Investors reach foreign shares through direct purchases, dual listings or depositary receipts; dual-class structures split economic and voting rights; and voting runs through proxies, with proxy advisors and asset managers shaping outcomes on behalf of asset owners.
mins: 20

## Investing across jurisdictions
- **Direct investing**: buy shares on the foreign exchange in the local currency. Brings currency, custody, tax and regulatory complexity.
- **Dual listing**: the issuer lists on a second exchange.
- **Depositary receipts (DRs)**: a bank holds the foreign shares and issues receipts that trade in the investor's market and currency.
  - **ADRs** trade in the US; **GDRs** trade outside the US and the issuer's home market.
  - **Sponsored** (the issuer participates; DR holders usually get voting rights) vs. **unsponsored** (no issuer involvement; the depository keeps the votes).
  - Holders still bear currency risk: the DR's price tracks the foreign share price converted at the exchange rate.
- **Indirect** routes: funds, ETFs, and index products.

## Dual-class shares
Two (or more) classes with the same economic rights but different votes: for example class B with 10 votes per share held by founders, class A with one vote sold to the public. Founders keep control with a minority of the capital. Benefits: long-term focus, protection from short-term pressure. Risks: weaker accountability, entrenched management. Some companies use **sunset clauses** that convert super-voting shares over time; some index providers limit or exclude dual-class companies.

## The voting process
1. The company sets a **record date**; holders on that date can vote.
2. It sends **proxy materials** with the items to vote on.
3. **Management proposals**: elect directors, appoint the auditor, approve pay ("say on pay"), mergers, share issuance.
4. **Shareholder proposals**: often on governance, climate, social issues.
5. Votes are cast in person or by **proxy**; results are tallied at the meeting.

Who's involved:
- **Board and management**: set the agenda and recommend votes.
- **Proxy advisors** (research firms): analyze items and recommend votes; influential with institutions.
- **Asset managers**: vote shares they manage, often under stewardship codes and their voting policy.
- **Asset owners** (pension funds, endowments): may set their own policy or delegate.
- A **proxy contest** is when a dissident shareholder solicits votes to replace directors.

# v5m3
take: New shares are sold in the primary market (IPOs, follow-ons, rights offerings); existing shares trade on exchanges, off-exchange venues or OTC; liquidity is judged by spreads, float and average daily volume; indexes differ by weighting and scope.
mins: 25

## Primary market
- **IPO**: first public sale, usually underwritten with **book building** (the bank gauges demand, sets the price, allocates shares). Alternatives: **direct listing** (no new shares, no underwriter) and merging with a **SPAC**.
- **Seasoned (follow-on) offering**: more shares from a listed company; dilutes existing holders.
- **Rights offering**: existing holders get the right to buy new shares, usually at a discount, so they can avoid dilution.
- **Private placement**: sold to selected investors.

## Secondary market venues
| Venue | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Exchange | Order-driven: a central limit order book; opening and closing call auctions | Most liquid, transparent; most volume |
| Off-exchange | Broker internalization, dark pools, alternative trading systems; large **block trades** negotiated by brokers | Very large or very small trades; less market impact |
| OTC | Quote-driven: dealers post bid and ask prices | Smaller, less liquid companies |

## Liquidity measures
- **Bid-ask spread**: narrower is more liquid.
- **Float**: shares actually available to trade = shares outstanding − closely held shares (insiders, governments, strategic holders, restricted shares).
- **Average daily volume (ADV)**: average shares traded per day.
- **Market depth**: how much can trade near the current price.

Example: 200m shares outstanding; founders hold 50m and a strategic partner 30m. Float = 120m. ADV is 1.5m shares. A fund wanting to buy 6m shares while staying at 20% of daily volume needs about 6 / (0.20 × 1.5) = **20 trading days**.

## Equity indexes
| Weighting | Weight proportional to | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Price-weighted | Share price | High-priced stocks dominate; splits change weights |
| Market-cap weighted | Market cap | Most common; large companies dominate |
| Float-adjusted market cap | Float × price | The standard for broad indexes |
| Equal-weighted | Same for all | Small-cap tilt; needs frequent rebalancing |
| Fundamental | Sales, earnings, book value, dividends | Value tilt |

Scopes: broad market, size (large, mid, small), style (value, growth), sector, country and region. Uses: gauging the market, benchmarking managers, and building index funds and ETFs.

# v5m4
take: Shareholders earn through dividends, buybacks and price change; splits change share counts not wealth; the price drops by about the dividend on the ex-date; and holding period return = (price change + dividends) / starting price.
mins: 20

## Distributions
- **Cash dividends**: regular or special; taxable income for most holders.
- **Share repurchases**: open-market purchases, tender offers, Dutch auctions, or direct negotiation. Similar wealth effect to dividends, but more flexible (no expectation of repeating), may be taxed more lightly, and can signal management thinks shares are cheap. Buybacks reduce shares outstanding and can lift EPS.
- **Stock dividends and splits**: more shares, proportionally lower price; a 2-for-1 split doubles shares and halves the price. **Reverse splits** do the opposite (often to keep a price above listing minimums). No change to the company's value or your ownership share.

## Dividend chronology
| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| Declaration | Board announces amount and dates |
| Ex-dividend | From this date, buyers don't get the dividend; the price typically drops by about the dividend |
| Record (holder-of-record) | Shareholders on the books are entitled to the dividend; set by the settlement cycle relative to the ex-date |
| Payment | Cash is paid |

## Returns
A shareholder earns in two ways: the share price changes, and the company may pay dividends. The **price return** counts only the first; the **total return** counts both.

```
Price return = (P1 − P0) / P0
Total (holding period) return = (P1 − P0 + D) / P0
```
~ P0 :: the price paid at the start
~ P1 :: the price at the end (or the sale price)
~ D :: dividends received during the holding period
Example: buy at $50, receive $1.50 in dividends, sell at $54.
- Price return = 4 / 50 = **8.0%**.
- Total return = (4 + 1.50) / 50 = **11.0%**.

With **reinvested dividends**, buy more shares on each payment date; the total return compounds. Over multiple periods, link returns: (1 + R1)(1 + R2)... − 1.

## Price as present value
A share is worth the present value of what you expect to receive: dividends while you hold it, plus the sale price.
```
P0 = D1/(1 + r) + ... + (Dn + Pn)/(1 + r)^n
```
~ D1 ... Dn :: dividends expected in years 1 to n
~ Pn :: the price you expect to sell at in year n
~ r :: the return investors require on the stock

# v5m5
take: Price is what the market pays; intrinsic value is what the cash flows are worth; compare market cap to book value and use enterprise value (equity + debt − cash) to compare firms with different capital structures; absolute models estimate value directly, relative models compare multiples.
mins: 25

## Price, value and perceived value
market price :: what the share trades at; reflects the views of all active participants
intrinsic value :: the value of expected future cash flows; unobservable, estimated
perceived value :: one investor's estimate of intrinsic value

If you think intrinsic value is above the price, the stock looks undervalued: investors buy, and the company might buy back shares. If below, investors sell, and the company might issue shares. Prices move toward value as information spreads, but mispricing can persist.

## Observable measures
**Book value of equity**: assets minus liabilities at accounting values. Backward-looking; misses internally generated intangibles.

**Market capitalization** = share price × shares outstanding. Comparing it to book (**price-to-book**) shows how much the market values future cash-generating potential beyond net assets.

**Enterprise value (EV)**: the cost of buying the whole business.
```
EV = Market cap + Debt + Preferred stock + Minority interest − Cash and investments
```
~ EV :: enterprise value
~ Market cap :: share price × shares outstanding
~ Debt :: the company's borrowings (market value if available)
~ Minority interest :: the part of subsidiaries owned by outside shareholders
~ Cash and investments :: subtracted because a buyer gets them with the company
Example: market cap $8bn, debt $3bn, cash $1bn. EV = **$10bn**.

EV is less affected by capital structure, so EV-based multiples (EV/EBITDA) compare companies with different leverage better than price-based ones (P/E).

## Valuation models
| Approach | Examples | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absolute: present value | Dividend discount, FCFE, FCFF, residual income | Directly estimates intrinsic value from fundamentals | Many assumptions; sensitive to discount rate and growth |
| Absolute: asset-based | Sum of asset values minus liabilities | Good for resource firms, holding companies, liquidation | Hard for firms whose value is in intangibles or growth |
| Relative | P/E, P/B, P/S, EV/EBITDA versus peers | Simple, market-based, easy to compare | Assumes peers are fairly priced; may miss company specifics |

Analysts often use several methods and reconcile them.

= b
why: Intrinsic value above market price suggests undervaluation.

# v5m6
take: Value = present value of future cash flows: dividends, FCFE, FCFF (then subtract debt), or book value plus residual income; Gordon growth V0 = D1/(r − g); use multistage models for uneven growth; plain preferred is D/r.
mins: 35

## Which cash flow
| Model | Cash flow | Discount at | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dividend discount (DDM) | Dividends | Cost of equity | Steady dividend payers |
| FCFE | Free cash flow to equity | Cost of equity | Non-payers, or payout ≠ capacity |
| FCFF | Free cash flow to the firm | WACC; subtract debt to get equity | Leveraged firms, changing capital structures |
| Residual income | NI − (cost of equity × beginning book) | Cost of equity | Firms with no dividends or negative FCF; book-value-heavy firms |

```
Residual income_t = Net income_t − r × Book value_(t−1)
Value = Book value_0 + PV of future residual income
```
~ Residual income :: profit above the cost of the shareholders' capital used to earn it
~ Net income_t :: profit in year t
~ r :: the cost of equity (the return shareholders require)
~ Book value_(t−1) :: shareholders' equity at the start of year t
~ Book value_0 :: shareholders' equity today

## Constant growth (Gordon)
If a company's dividend grows at a steady rate forever, the present value of all those dividends collapses into one simple formula, the **Gordon growth model**.

```
V0 = D1 / (r − g)      where D1 = D0 × (1 + g)
```
~ V0 :: the share's value today
~ D0 :: the dividend just paid
~ D1 :: next year's expected dividend
~ r :: the required return on equity
~ g :: the constant growth rate of dividends forever (must be below r)
Needs r > g, and g sustainable forever (not above long-run economic growth).

Example: D0 = $2.00, g = 4%, r = 9%. D1 = 2.08. V0 = 2.08 / 0.05 = **$41.60**.

The same formula works with FCFE (V0 = FCFE1/(r − g)) and FCFF (firm value = FCFF1/(WACC − g)).

## Multistage
When near-term growth is high, forecast each year, then use a terminal value when growth settles.

Example: D0 = $1.00. Dividends grow 20% for two years, then 5% forever. r = 10%.
- D1 = 1.20; D2 = 1.44; D3 = 1.512.
- Terminal value at year 2 = D3 / (r − g) = 1.512 / 0.05 = 30.24.
- V0 = 1.20/1.10 + (1.44 + 30.24)/1.10² = 1.091 + 26.182 = **$27.27**.

Terminal values usually dominate the total, so small changes in r or g swing the value.

## Limitations
- **Sensitivity**: value explodes as r − g narrows.
- **Base year**: starting from an unusual year distorts everything.
- **Static assumptions**: constant growth and margins rarely hold; companies move through life cycles.
- **Discount rate inputs** (risk-free rate, risk premium, beta) shift with macro conditions.

## Preferred stock
Non-callable, non-convertible perpetual preferred:
```
V0 = Dividend / r
```
~ Dividend :: the fixed preferred dividend per year
~ r :: the return investors require on the preferred shares
Example: $5 annual dividend, required return 8%: **$62.50**.

With a maturity: PV of dividends + PV of par. Features that help the **issuer** (call) lower value; features that help the **investor** (put, conversion, cumulative dividends) raise it.

b) $62.50
c) $64.00
= b
why: 4 / 0.064 = $62.50.

a) discounts FCFF at the cost of equity

take: Value a stock by applying a peer multiple (comparables) or a multiple justified by its own fundamentals; match numerators to denominators (price with earnings, EV with EBITDA); pick peers by industry or by risk factors; and use forward multiples for growth and exit multiples for terminal value.
mins: 30

## Two ways to use multiples
**Method of comparables**: compare the company's multiple with peers' (median or mean). If its P/E is well below peers with similar growth and risk, it may be undervalued.

**Method of forecasted fundamentals** (justified multiples): derive the multiple from a DCF model.
```
Justified forward P/E = (D1/E1) / (r − g) = Payout ratio / (r − g)
Justified P/B = (ROE − g) / (r − g)
```
~ P/E :: price-to-earnings ratio
~ D1 / E1 :: next year's dividend / next year's earnings = the payout ratio
~ r :: required return on equity
~ g :: long-run growth rate
~ P/B :: price-to-book ratio
~ ROE :: return on equity
Example: payout 40%, r = 10%, g = 5%. Justified forward P/E = 0.40 / 0.05 = **8.0×**.
Example: ROE 15%, g 5%, r 10%. Justified P/B = 0.10 / 0.05 = **2.0×**.

Higher growth, higher ROE and lower risk justify higher multiples.

## The multiples
| Numerator | Denominator | Multiple | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | EPS | P/E | Profitable companies; widely used |
| Price | Book value per share | P/B | Banks, insurers, asset-heavy firms; works when earnings are negative |
| Price | Sales per share | P/S | Early-stage or loss-making firms; sales are harder to manipulate |
| Price | Cash flow per share | P/CF | Where earnings are distorted by non-cash items |
| EV | EBITDA | EV/EBITDA | Comparing firms with different leverage and depreciation |
| EV | Sales | EV/Sales | Loss-making or early-stage firms |

**Match the claim**: equity measures (price) go with equity-holder metrics (EPS, book equity); firm measures (EV) go with all-capital metrics (EBITDA, EBIT, sales). P/E on EBITDA would be a mismatch.

Industry-specific denominators exist too: EV per subscriber, price per barrel of reserves, EV per bed.

## Picking peers
- **Industry-based**: same classification (GICS sector or sub-industry), similar business mix, size and geography.
- **Factor-based**: companies with similar exposure to risk factors (market beta, size, value, growth), even across industries.

Use **current** multiples for today's value, **past** multiples to see trends relative to the industry, and **projected** (forward) multiples for growth companies. A **terminal value** in a DCF can be an exit multiple × the final year's metric.

## Traps
! Trailing P/E is distorted by one-off items and cyclical peaks or troughs.
! P/E is meaningless with negative earnings; use P/S or EV/Sales.
! Comparables only show relative value; if the sector is overpriced, so is your estimate.

= b

= c
why: Price is an equity value; EBITDA belongs to all capital providers.

= b

take: A disaggregated model builds revenue, costs, assets and financing separately so each assumption tells part of the story; the right level of detail depends on the life-cycle stage; valuation runs from historicals to forecasts, free cash flow, terminal value and present value, with distress handled by probability-weighting scenarios.
mins: 30

## Why disaggregate
A single growth rate hides the story. Breaking the model into parts (revenue by segment and driver, costs by type, assets by need, financing by source) makes each assumption visible, testable and easier to explain.

## Building the parts
- **Revenue**: top-down (market size × market share) or bottom-up (volume × price, by product, customer or region). Usually both, cross-checked.
- **Expenses and profit**: margins from history, adjusted for scale, mix and competition; separate fixed and variable costs.
- **Assets**: working capital from days (DSO, DOH, days payables) applied to sales and COGS; capex and depreciation for fixed assets.
- **Financing**: debt issued and repaid, interest, dividends, buybacks; the balance sheet must balance.

## Life cycle and model choice
| Stage | Focus | Typical valuation |
|---|---|---|
| Start-up | Market size, adoption, cash burn | Scenarios, EV/Sales, probability-weighted outcomes |
| Growth | Revenue drivers, scaling margins | FCFF with long explicit forecast |
| Mature | Margins, capex discipline, payouts | DDM, FCFE, P/E, EV/EBITDA |
| Decline or distress | Cash generation, asset values, survival | Liquidation value, probability-weighted scenarios |

## The valuation steps
1. Gather historical statements.
2. Forecast the statements over the explicit horizon.
3. Derive **free cash flow** (FCFF or FCFE) from the forecasts.
4. Estimate a **terminal value** (growth model or exit multiple).
5. Discount everything to the present.

Then test it: change key assumptions (growth, margins, discount rate) to see how sensitive the value is.

## Financial distress
If the company might fail, weight the outcomes:
```
Value = P(going concern) × Going-concern value + P(distress) × Liquidation value
```
~ P(going concern) :: the probability the company survives and keeps operating
~ Going-concern value :: its value if it survives
~ P(distress) :: the probability it fails (1 − P(going concern))
~ Liquidation value :: what shareholders get if it is wound up
Example: 80% chance it survives with a $50 value, 20% chance of liquidation at $10. Value = 0.8 × 50 + 0.2 × 10 = **$42**.

## The process
1. **Define the industry**: what products, what customers, what geography.
2. **Survey** it: size, growth, profitability, market shares.
3. **Evaluate structure**: Porter's five forces.
4. **Assess external influences**: PESTLE.
5. **Evaluate competitive strategy** of the companies within it.

## Classification
Third-party systems (GICS, ICB and others) group companies by **principal business activity**, usually by revenue: sector → industry group → industry → sub-industry.

Limitations: conglomerates span several industries; classifications lag changes in a business; companies in the same group can differ hugely in size and model; regional differences.

Alternatives: by **business-cycle sensitivity** (cyclical vs. defensive), **statistical** grouping (stocks whose returns move together), or **geography**.

## Industry survey
- **Size**: total revenue, total addressable market.
- **Growth** and **life-cycle stage**: embryonic, growth, shakeout, mature, decline.
- **Profitability**: margins and returns on capital across the industry.
- **Concentration**: how much of the market the leaders hold.
```
Concentration ratio (N) = combined market share of the N largest firms
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) = sum of squared market shares (in %)
```
~ N :: the number of largest firms counted (often 4 or 8)
~ Market share :: a firm's sales / total industry sales, in %
~ HHI :: Herfindahl-Hirschman Index; ranges up to 10,000 for a monopoly
Example: shares of 40%, 30%, 20% and 10%. HHI = 1,600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = **3,000**. Higher HHI means more concentrated, usually less price competition.

## Porter's five forces and moats
Threat of **new entrants**, threat of **substitutes**, **buyer** power, **supplier** power, and **rivalry**. Weak forces leave room for high, lasting profits.

Sources of an **economic moat** (lasting advantage):
- **Intangible assets**: brands, patents, licenses.
- **Switching costs**: customers find it costly to leave.
- **Network effects**: the product gets more valuable as more people use it.
- **Cost advantages**: scale, processes, location.
- **Efficient scale**: a market only big enough for a few players.

## PESTLE
**Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental** factors that shift an industry's growth and the share each competitor can win.

## Competitive strategy
- **Start-ups and growth firms**: build the product, size the market, win customers.
- **Established firms** (Porter's generic strategies):
  - **Cost leadership**: lowest cost producer; wins on price.
  - **Differentiation**: unique product or brand; charges a premium.
  - **Focus**: cost or differentiation within a narrow segment.
- **Declining firms**: harvest cash, consolidate, or exit.

## Revenue and profitability
Revenue: **top-down** (economy → industry → market share) plus **bottom-up** (volume, price, mix, geography).

| Measure | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | Complete | Hard to compare across firms (financing, taxes, one-offs) |
| Operating income | Core operations | Affected by different investment and depreciation policies |
| EBITDA | Before discretionary spending, depreciation | Not standardized; often "adjusted" by management |

**Pricing power**: the ability to raise prices without losing much volume (low price elasticity), and to pass on cost inflation.

**Operating leverage**: high fixed costs magnify the effect of sales changes on operating profit.
```
Degree of operating leverage = %ΔEBIT / %ΔSales = Contribution margin / EBIT
```
~ EBIT :: earnings before interest and taxes (operating profit)
~ %ΔEBIT, %ΔSales :: percentage changes
~ Contribution margin :: sales minus variable costs: what's left to cover fixed costs
Example: sales $1,000, variable costs $600, fixed costs $250. Contribution = 400; EBIT = 150. DOL = 400 / 150 = **2.67**: a 10% rise in sales lifts EBIT about 26.7%.

## Capital use
**Working capital**: the operating cycle (days of inventory + days receivables) and the cash conversion cycle (minus days payables). Shorter is better, but it varies hugely by industry.

**Capital investment**: size and timing of capex and acquisitions; capex above depreciation signals growth.
```
ROIC = NOPAT / Invested capital
```
~ ROIC :: return on invested capital
~ NOPAT :: net operating profit after tax = EBIT × (1 − tax rate)
~ Invested capital :: the debt and equity tied up in operations
A company creates value when **ROIC > WACC**.

**Capital structure**: the mix of debt and equity, judged by leverage, coverage and the company's ability to withstand a downturn.

## CAPM and the market model
The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) says investors should earn the risk-free rate plus extra return only for market (systematic) risk. Beta measures how much of that risk a stock carries.

```
CAPM: E(Ri) = Rf + βi × [E(Rm) − Rf]
Market model: Ri = αi + βi × Rm + εi
```
~ E(Ri) :: the expected (required) return on stock i
~ Rf :: the risk-free rate
~ βi :: beta: how much the stock moves for a 1% market move
~ E(Rm) − Rf :: the market risk premium (MRP): extra return expected for holding the market
~ αi :: alpha, the regression intercept
~ εi :: the error term: stock-specific moves
Beta measures sensitivity to the market; it's usually estimated by regressing the stock's returns on the market's (the market model). Example: Rf = 4%, β = 1.2, market risk premium (MRP) = 5%. Cost of equity = 4 + 1.2 × 5 = **10.0%**.

## Estimating beta
Choices change the answer: the index used, the return frequency (daily, weekly, monthly) and the period length. Raw betas tend to drift toward 1 over time, so some analysts **adjust** them (a common version: adjusted beta = 2/3 × raw beta + 1/3 × 1.0). For private or newly listed companies, use peers' betas (adjusted for leverage).

## Estimating the market risk premium
- **Historical**: average past excess returns. Depends on the period and on arithmetic vs. geometric averaging.
- **Forward-looking**: implied by current prices, e.g. dividend yield + expected growth − risk-free rate.
- **Surveys** of investors or experts.

## Country risk premium
For emerging markets, add a **country risk premium (CRP)** to the MRP:
```
CRP = Sovereign yield spread × (σ of the country's equity market / σ of its bond market)
E(Ri) = Rf + βi × (MRP + CRP)
```
~ CRP :: country risk premium
~ Sovereign yield spread :: the country's government bond yield minus a developed-market benchmark yield
~ σ :: standard deviation (volatility)
~ MRP :: market risk premium
Example: sovereign spread 3%, equity volatility 30%, bond volatility 20%. CRP = 3% × 1.5 = 4.5%. With Rf 4%, β 1.2, MRP 5%: cost of equity = 4 + 1.2 × 9.5 = **15.4%**.

## APT and multi-factor models
**Arbitrage pricing theory** allows several systematic risk factors:
```
E(Ri) = Rf + βi1 × λ1 + βi2 × λ2 + ... + βik × λk
```
~ βi1 ... βik :: the stock's sensitivity to each of k risk factors
~ λ1 ... λk :: the extra return (risk premium) the market pays for each factor ("lambda")
λ = the risk premium for each factor; β = the stock's sensitivity to it. Factors aren't specified by the theory.

In practice:
- **Macroeconomic** factors: GDP growth surprises, inflation, interest rates, credit spreads.
- **Fundamental** factors: size, value, momentum, quality (e.g., the Fama-French models: market, size, value, plus profitability and investment).
- **Statistical** factors from the data.

A stock with high exposure to a priced factor (say, small size) will have a higher expected return than CAPM alone suggests.
