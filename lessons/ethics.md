## The idea in plain English
Clients hand investment professionals their money because they can't check the work themselves. That gap in expertise is why the industry needs more than laws: it needs a profession whose members promise, in public, to put clients first and to keep their skills sharp.

A **profession** is an occupation with specialized knowledge, a commitment to serve others, and a shared code of ethics that members agree to follow. That last part is what separates a profession from a trade body or craft guild.

profession :: specialized knowledge + service to others + a shared code of ethics, policed by a recognized body
code of ethics :: public statement of a profession's principles and the conduct expected of members
standards of conduct :: the specific, minimum behaviors members must follow; benchmarks for "minimally acceptable"
fiduciary duty :: the obligation to act in the client's best interest with a high standard of care

## Why the bar is higher than the law
Laws are mostly rules: they say what is required or forbidden. Ethics asks what is right, balancing self-interest against the effects on others. Something can be legal but unethical (trading on inside information is legal in some countries; CFA Institute treats it as unethical), and occasionally a law can conflict with what ethics requires.

Unethical behavior costs firms twice: clients leave (lower revenue) and regulators investigate (higher costs).

## Why good people slip
- **Overconfidence** in your own morality: "I'm honest, so this must be fine."
- **Situational influences**: pressure from bosses, money, prestige, loyalty to the team. They are external, short-term and easy to underestimate.
- **Short-term focus**: weighing the immediate payoff over long-run consequences.
- A **compliance-only mindset** ("is it allowed?") can oversimplify decisions and itself becomes a situational influence.

! The curriculum's fix for situational influences is to consciously consider long-term consequences, not to add more compliance rules.

## The decision framework
| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| Identify | The facts, the stakeholders and duties owed, the ethical principles at stake, any conflicts of interest |
| Consider | Situational influences, extra guidance (firm policy, a mentor, compliance), alternative actions |
| Act | Make the decision and carry it out |
| Reflect | Did it turn out as expected? What would you do differently? |

The process is iterative: you can loop back. When a dilemma shows up at work, raising it with a senior person inside the firm is usually the right first move.

## How enforcement works
The **Professional Conduct (PC) staff** investigates; the **Disciplinary Review Committee (DRC)**, a panel of volunteer charterholders, hears contested cases and reviews agreed ones. The Board of Governors oversees both.

Investigations start from four places: your own disclosure on the annual **Professional Conduct Statement** (litigation, criminal charges, written complaints), written complaints, public sources such as the press or regulators, and exam monitoring.

After investigating, PC staff can close the case with no sanction, issue a **cautionary letter**, or pursue discipline. The member or candidate can accept or reject the charges; if rejected, a DRC hearing panel decides.

| Sanction | Who |
|---|---|
| Public censure | Members |
| Suspension of membership and of the right to use the CFA designation | Members |
| Revocation of membership and the designation | Members |
| Suspension or ban from the CFA Program | Candidates |

## The Code of Ethics: six principles
Members and candidates must:
- Act with **integrity, competence, diligence and respect**, ethically, toward everyone in the markets.
- Put the **integrity of the profession and clients' interests above their own**.
- Use **reasonable care and independent judgment**.
- **Practice and encourage others** to practice professionally and ethically.
- **Promote the integrity and viability of the global capital markets** for the ultimate benefit of society.
- **Maintain and improve their competence** and help others do the same.

## The seven Standards
| Standard | Sub-sections |
|---|---|
| I Professionalism | A Knowledge of the Law · B Independence and Objectivity · C Misrepresentation · D Misconduct · E Competence |
| II Integrity of Capital Markets | A Material Nonpublic Information · B Market Manipulation |
| III Duties to Clients | A Loyalty, Prudence, and Care · B Fair Dealing · C Suitability · D Performance Presentation · E Preservation of Confidentiality |
| IV Duties to Employers | A Loyalty · B Additional Compensation Arrangements · C Responsibilities of Supervisors |
| V Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions | A Diligence and Reasonable Basis · B Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients · C Record Retention |
| VI Conflicts of Interest | A Avoid or Disclose Conflicts · B Priority of Transactions · C Referral Fees |
| VII Responsibilities as a CFA Institute Member or CFA Candidate | A Conduct as Participants in CFA Institute Programs · B Reference to CFA Institute, the CFA Designation, and the CFA Program |

> Count check: 5 + 2 + 5 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 = 23 sub-standards.

## What changed in 2023
- **I(E) Competence (new):** act with and maintain the competence your role requires. No specific continuing-education program is required; it depends on the facts of your job.
- **V(B) Communication (revised):** you must now disclose the **nature of the services** you provide and the **costs** to the client of those services.
- **VI(A) Avoid or Disclose Conflicts (renamed and revised):** avoid conflicts where reasonably possible; when you can't, disclose them prominently and in plain language.

## Firms and the Code
The Code and Standards bind individuals, not firms. A firm can adopt them and claim compliance using CFA Institute's wording, which states that the claim **has not been verified by CFA Institute**. The separate **Asset Manager Code** is written for firms.

# v10m3

mins: 30

## I(A) Knowledge of the Law
Know the laws, rules and regulations that govern your work, including the Code and Standards. **When they conflict, follow the stricter one.**

| Your home law | Where you work | What you follow |
|---|---|---|
| Stricter than the Code | Any | The law |
| Less strict than the Code | Any | The Code |
| No law on the issue | Any | The Code |

If you learn of a violation: first raise it with your supervisor or compliance; if it isn't fixed, **dissociate** (remove your name from the report, decline the work, and possibly resign). Staying quiet while you keep working on it counts as participating. You're encouraged, but **not required**, to report violations to CFA Institute or regulators, though the law may require it.

## I(B) Independence and Objectivity
Don't offer, solicit or accept anything that could reasonably be expected to compromise your own or someone else's independence.
- **Modest gifts and customary entertainment** are fine.
- **Gifts from clients** are less of a threat than gifts from companies you cover or brokers seeking business, but disclose them to your employer. A gift tied to future performance is additional compensation and needs consent under IV(B).
- **Issuer-paid research**: accept only a **flat fee** not tied to the conclusion, and disclose it.
- **Travel**: use commercial transport and pay your own way when you can; avoid lavish trips paid by issuers.
- Don't let investment banking colleagues, big clients or fund sponsors pressure a rating or recommendation.

## I(C) Misrepresentation
Don't knowingly mislead about analysis, recommendations, performance, qualifications or services. That includes:
- **Guaranteeing** returns on risky investments. Describing a product that genuinely has a guarantee (an insured deposit, a principal-protected note) is fine.
- **Plagiarism**: using others' research, models or words without credit. You may use facts from recognized statistical sources without citation, and research done by colleagues at your firm may go out under the firm's name.
- Overstating credentials or the firm's capabilities, including on social media, and making unsupported ESG or impact claims.
- **Omissions** that leave a misleading picture.

## I(D) Misconduct
No dishonesty, fraud or deceit in professional conduct, and nothing that reflects adversely on your professional reputation, integrity or competence. Purely personal matters (a lawful protest, a personal bankruptcy with no fraud) are generally not violations. Drinking at lunch that impairs your work, or a conviction for fraud, are.

## I(E) Competence (new in 2023)
Act with and maintain the knowledge and skills your role requires. There's **no required program**: competence is judged by the facts of your job. If a task is outside your skills, get training, bring in someone who has them, or decline it.

## Worked case
An analyst is told by her firm's investment banking head that a "sell" on a client company "won't help the relationship." She keeps her sell rating and documents the conversation.
- I(B): she resisted pressure, so no violation.
- If she had softened the rating to "hold" without new analysis, that would violate I(B), and possibly I(C) if the report then misrepresents her view.

## Traps
! "Reporting violations to regulators" is not required by the Code. Dissociation is.
! Dissociating does not always mean quitting; resignation is the last resort.
! A gift from a client is not automatically a violation. A gift from a company you cover that could sway your rating is.

## II(A) Material Nonpublic Information
Two tests:
material :: a reasonable investor would want to know it, or it would likely move the price. The more specific and reliable the source, the more material.
nonpublic :: not yet disseminated to the marketplace. Telling a group of analysts at a meeting is not public; a press release or regulatory filing is.

If you have MNPI: **don't act on it and don't cause others to act** (no tipping, not even hints). Encourage the company to make it public.

**Mosaic theory.** You may reach a conclusion by combining public information with nonpublic but **nonmaterial** pieces, even if the conclusion itself would be material had the company announced it. Example: an analyst counts trucks at a factory, reads public filings and talks to suppliers, then concludes sales will miss. That's legitimate research.

Other points:
- An **analyst's own recommendation** can itself be material and nonpublic before it's released, so don't trade ahead of it.
- **Expert networks**: if an expert shares MNPI, you can't use it.
- If you receive MNPI by accident (a misdirected email), the same rule applies.

Firm compliance tools: **firewalls** between banking and research or sales, **watch lists** (monitored securities), **restricted lists** (no trading or recommending), limits on proprietary and personal trading, and press releases to get information out.

## II(B) Market Manipulation
Don't distort prices or artificially inflate volume **with intent to mislead**.
- **Information-based**: spreading false rumors, "pump and dump," misleading research to move a price you'll profit from.
- **Transaction-based**: wash trades (buying and selling to yourself), trading to set an artificial price at the close, cornering a security to squeeze others.

Intent is the line: legitimate strategies that exploit differences in information or market power are not manipulation, and trading for real reasons (liquidity, tax, hedging) is fine.

## Worked case
A portfolio manager overhears a CEO tell his lawyer, on a train, that the company will miss earnings badly. The CEO is a reliable source, the news is specific, and it isn't public: it's MNPI. The manager can't sell, can't tell a colleague to sell, and should not trade the stock until the news is released.

## Traps
! Information shared at an analyst meeting, a conference call for selected investors, or by a company insider in private is still nonpublic.
! Mosaic conclusions are allowed even if they turn out to be "material": the test is that each nonpublic piece was nonmaterial.
! Thinly traded stocks make transaction-based manipulation easier. "I didn't intend to mislead" must be credible from the facts.

## Quick check

b) act and recommend accordingly, under the mosaic theory
c) act only after the company confirms the cut
= b
why: The mosaic theory allows conclusions built from public information and nonmaterial nonpublic information.

a) Buying a stock you believe is undervalued after research
b) Trading the same stock back and forth between accounts you control to create the look of volume
c) Selling a large position over several days to limit price impact
= b
why: Wash trades create artificial volume to mislead other participants.

a) stop employees and the firm from trading or recommending certain securities
b) watch trading in securities for unusual activity without restricting it
c) list clients who can't receive research
= a
why: A restricted list blocks trading and recommendations; a watch list only monitors.

a) II(A), because the pending recommendation is material nonpublic information
b) II(B) only
c) No standard, since the trader didn't write the report
= a
why: An influential analyst's unreleased recommendation can be material and nonpublic; trading ahead of it violates II(A).

## III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care
Act for the client's benefit and put clients ahead of your employer and yourself. First identify who the client is:
- For an individual account: the person.
- For a pension plan: the **beneficiaries**, not the company sponsor.
- For a fund run to a mandate: the fund, managed to its stated strategy.

Points that come up:
- **Soft dollars**: brokerage commissions belong to the client. Use them only for research that benefits the client, and seek **best execution**.
- **Proxy voting**: vote in clients' interest. You don't have to vote every proxy if the cost outweighs the benefit, but have a policy.
- Keep client assets in custody, separate from the firm's.

## III(B) Fair Dealing
Treat all clients **fairly, not necessarily identically**.
- Disseminate recommendations and changes so that all clients get them at about the same time; don't tip favored clients first.
- **Allocate trades fairly**, typically pro rata, including oversubscribed IPOs. Don't favor family members' or your own accounts.
- Different service levels (premium service) are fine if disclosed and available to everyone willing to pay. Investment results must not be worse for some clients because of it.

## III(C) Suitability
In an advisory relationship:
- Learn the client's **objectives** (return and risk) and **constraints**: liquidity, time horizon, taxes, legal and regulatory, unique circumstances. Write them in an **investment policy statement (IPS)**.
- Update it **regularly** (at least annually) and when circumstances change.
- Judge each investment **in the context of the whole portfolio**.

When a client asks for a trade that isn't suitable (an unsolicited request): explain why it's unsuitable and document it. If it's small relative to the portfolio, you can generally execute it; if it would materially change the portfolio, discuss updating the IPS or have the client open a separate account.

When managing a fund to a **mandate or style**, invest only in what fits the stated strategy, even if something else looks attractive.

## III(D) Performance Presentation
Make performance **fair, accurate and complete**:
- Don't show only your best accounts; include terminated accounts in composites.
- Label simulated or backtested results as such.
- Say whether returns are gross or net of fees.
- Results from a previous firm can be used only if you can support them and you disclose where they came from.
- Complying with the GIPS standards is the recommended way to meet this standard.

## III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality
Keep information about **current, former and prospective** clients confidential unless:
- it concerns **illegal activity** by the client,
- **disclosure is required by law**, or
- the **client permits** it.
Sharing information with CFA Institute's Professional Conduct staff during an investigation is not a violation.

## Worked case
A manager runs a "large-cap value" fund. A small-cap growth stock she likes is surging. Adding it violates III(C)(2) because it's outside the mandate, even if it would help returns. If she thinks the mandate is too narrow, the fix is to propose a change through proper channels, not to drift.

## Traps
! Fair dealing does not require equal treatment, only fair treatment with disclosed service levels.
! The pension plan's client is the beneficiaries, not the sponsor's management.
! Confidentiality covers former and prospective clients too.

b) III(B) only
c) No standard if the commissions are competitive
= a
why: Commissions are client assets; using them for research that doesn't benefit the client breaches loyalty.

a) giving it first to the largest clients
b) giving it pro rata to all clients for whom it is suitable
c) giving it to clients who complained most recently
= b
why: Fair dealing calls for a fair allocation, typically pro rata, among clients for whom the IPO is suitable.

c) execute it without comment, since the client asked

## IV(A) Loyalty
In matters related to your job, act for your employer's benefit: don't deprive it of your skills, divulge its confidential information, or otherwise harm it.

**Leaving a firm.** You may prepare to leave (set up a new firm, register, rent an office) as long as you don't breach your duties while still employed. While employed you must not:
- solicit current clients or colleagues to move with you,
- take client lists, records or models, in paper or electronic form,
- misappropriate trade secrets.

After leaving you may contact former clients using **public information** and what you remember, unless a valid non-compete or non-solicitation agreement says otherwise.

Also:
- **Independent practice** for pay that competes with your employer needs notice and consent.
- **Whistleblowing** to protect clients or market integrity can be acceptable even if it harms the employer; doing it for personal gain is not.
- The duty applies whether you're an employee or independent contractor. Firm social media accounts and contacts belong to the firm.

## IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements
Don't accept gifts, benefits or pay that compete with, or could create a conflict with, your employer's interest **unless you get written consent from all parties involved** (your employer, and the client if relevant). A client bonus tied to future performance is the classic case. Email counts as written consent.

## IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors
Make **reasonable efforts** so that everyone under your supervision, including people who aren't CFA members, complies with laws and the Code.
- You can delegate tasks, not responsibility.
- If the firm's compliance system is inadequate, **decline in writing** to accept supervisory responsibility until reasonable procedures are in place.
- When you learn of a violation, respond promptly: investigate, increase supervision, restrict the person's activities. A warning alone is not enough for a serious breach.
- Watch for incentive structures that reward misconduct.

## Worked case
A client offers his portfolio manager a weekend at a resort if the portfolio beats the S&P 500 this year. Because it's tied to future performance and could create a conflict (the manager might take more risk in that account), the manager must get **written consent from her employer** before accepting. Disclosing after the fact is not enough.

## Traps
! Preparing to leave is allowed; soliciting before you leave is not.
! IV(B) needs written consent from all parties, not just disclosure.
! A supervisor isn't excused because the wrongdoer isn't a CFA member.

## V(A) Diligence and Reasonable Basis
Be **diligent, independent and thorough**, and have a **reasonable and adequate basis** supported by research for every analysis, recommendation or action. What counts as "reasonable" depends on the complexity of the investment and your role.
- **Third-party research**: check that it's sound (assumptions, rigor, timeliness, objectivity) before relying on it.
- **Quantitative models**: understand their assumptions and limits, and test them under stressed conditions.
- **Selecting external managers**: do due diligence and keep monitoring.
- **Group research**: if you disagree with a committee's conclusion but it had a reasonable basis and followed a sound process, you don't have to dissociate. You can record your dissent.

## V(B) Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients
Disclose:
1. the **nature of the services** you provide and the **costs** to the client (added in 2023),
2. the **basic format and general principles** of your investment process, and promptly any **material changes** to it,
3. **significant limitations and risks** of the process,
4. the factors important to your analysis, using reasonable judgment about which ones,
5. and **distinguish fact from opinion** (a projected EPS is an opinion, not a fact).

Short-form communications (a one-line rating, a tweet) are fine as long as the full analysis is available on request.

## V(C) Record Retention
Keep records that support your analysis, recommendations, actions and client communications. Records belong to the **firm**, so you can't take them when you leave. If no rule says how long, CFA Institute recommends **at least seven years**. Electronic records and notes from meetings count.

## Worked case
A firm switches its equity fund from a fundamental approach to a quantitative screening model. Under V(B) it must promptly tell clients about the change and the risks of the new process. Under V(A) the managers must understand the model's assumptions and test it before relying on it.

## Traps
! "We estimate EPS of $4.20" must be presented as an estimate, not as fact.
! Records you created still belong to your employer.
! Agreeing with a group decision you doubt is acceptable if the group's process was sound; you don't need to remove your name.

## VI(A) Avoid or Disclose Conflicts
Avoid, or fully and fairly disclose, anything that could reasonably be expected to impair your independence and objectivity or interfere with your duties to clients, prospects and employer. Since 2023 the standard says **avoid first**; when avoiding isn't reasonable, disclose. Disclosures must be **prominent, in plain language, and effective**: a line buried in fine print doesn't count.

Common conflicts to disclose:
- Owning shares in a company you recommend.
- Serving on a company's board.
- Your firm's investment banking relationship with a covered company.
- Compensation that rewards particular products or behaviors.
- Conflicts with your **employer** too, such as outside business or board seats.

## VI(B) Priority of Transactions
**Clients, then employer, then you.** Owning the same securities as clients is fine as long as clients aren't disadvantaged (for example, you don't trade ahead of them). Family accounts that are fee-paying clients are treated like any other client: neither favored nor shut out.

Firm practices that help:
- Limits on employee participation in **equity IPOs** and **private placements**.
- **Blackout or restricted periods** around client trades.
- **Reporting**: disclose holdings, send duplicate trade confirmations, **preclear** personal trades.

## VI(C) Referral Fees
Disclose to your **employer, clients and prospects** any compensation, consideration or benefit received from or paid to others for recommending products or services. Disclose the **nature and value**, and do it **before** the client enters the arrangement.

## Worked case
A wealth manager refers clients to an estate lawyer, who sends the manager tickets to sporting events in return. The tickets are a referral benefit: under VI(C) the manager must tell clients (and the employer) about the arrangement and its value before they engage the lawyer.

## Traps
! Disclosure buried in a 40-page document isn't "prominent."
! Owning what clients own is not a violation; trading ahead of them is.
! Referral fees go to the employer, clients and prospects, not just one of them.

## VII(A) Conduct as Participants in CFA Institute Programs
Don't compromise the reputation or integrity of CFA Institute, the designation, or the integrity, validity or security of its programs. Violations include:
- cheating, or using unauthorized materials;
- **disclosing confidential exam content**: specific questions, or which topics or formulas were or weren't tested, including on social media and forums;
- not following testing rules;
- giving false information to CFA Institute (for example on the Professional Conduct Statement);
- improperly using your role as a CFA Institute volunteer.

You may discuss the program in general terms and express opinions about CFA Institute, its policies or the exam's difficulty.

## VII(B) Reference to CFA Institute, the Designation, and the Program
Don't misrepresent or exaggerate what membership, the charter or candidacy means.

| Acceptable | Not acceptable |
|---|---|
| Jane Smith, CFA | Jane Smith, C.F.A. or Jane Smith, cfa |
| "a CFA charterholder" | "a CFA" (used as a noun) |
| "Level I candidate in the CFA Program" | "CFA Level I" or "CFA (expected 2028)" |
| "passed all three levels on the first attempt" (if true) | "charterholders achieve better returns" |
| "I passed Level II of the CFA Program" | a "partial" or "pending" designation |

To use the designation, a charterholder must keep paying **annual membership dues** and file the **Professional Conduct Statement**; otherwise they must stop using it until reinstated. You're a candidate from registration until results are released, or until you stop registering.

## Traps
! Saying which topics were not on your exam is still a leak.
! "CFA" is never a noun and never takes periods.
! Lapsed members can't use "CFA" after their name.

= b
why: Disclosing which topics were or weren't tested compromises exam integrity under VII(A).

## How to work an ethics question
1. **Who is the person?** Member or candidate (bound by the Code) or someone else.
2. **What is the relationship?** Client, employer, issuer, market.
3. **Which standard?** Name the most specific one, not just "professionalism."
4. **Is there a violation?** Read the exact facts. "Most likely" questions turn on one detail: consent, disclosure, timing, materiality.
5. **What's the fix?** Disclose, get consent, dissociate, decline, document.

## Situations that hit two or more standards
| Situation | Standards |
|---|---|
| Analyst trades after a CEO's slip at a dinner | II(A) MNPI; possibly VI(B) if ahead of clients |
| Manager leaves and takes the client list | IV(A) Loyalty; V(C) records belong to the firm; III(E) if client data is misused |
| Report shows only the best accounts' returns | III(D) Performance; I(C) Misrepresentation |
| Accepting a big gift from a covered company | I(B) Independence; VI(A) if undisclosed |
| Client bonus for beating a benchmark | IV(B) written consent; VI(A) disclosure |
| Supervisor ignores a junior's front-running | IV(C) Supervisors; VI(B) for the junior |
| Hot IPO goes to the manager's own account | III(B) Fair Dealing; VI(B) Priority |
| Fund drifts outside its mandate | III(C) Suitability; V(B) if the process change isn't disclosed |

## The usual "right answers"
- **Disclose** conflicts prominently, in plain language, before the client acts.
- **Get written consent** for additional compensation.
- **Dissociate** from violations; reporting to regulators is optional under the Code.
- **Follow the stricter** of law and Code.
- **Treat clients fairly**, allocate pro rata.
- **Distinguish fact from opinion.**

## Traps
! "Not a violation" is often the right answer. Don't assume wrongdoing; check each element of the standard.
! Good intentions don't fix a breach; consent or disclosure does.
! When two answers both describe violations, pick the one that matches the specific facts in the question.

## Quick check
? A manager allocates an oversubscribed IPO to her own account before clients get any. The standards most directly violated are:
a) III(B) Fair Dealing and VI(B) Priority of Transactions
b) II(B) Market Manipulation only
c) V(C) Record Retention
= a
why: Taking shares before clients is unfair allocation and puts personal trades ahead of client trades.

? An analyst leaves for a competitor and brings a spreadsheet of client contacts copied from the old firm's system. This most directly violates:
a) IV(A) Loyalty
b) III(D) Performance Presentation
c) VII(A) Conduct in CFA Institute Programs
= a
why: Taking employer records or client lists without permission breaches duty of loyalty to the employer; the records also belong to the firm under V(C).

? A candidate's firm gives clients a marketing deck showing a composite that excludes two accounts that lost money and were closed. This most likely violates:
a) III(D) Performance Presentation
b) VI(C) Referral Fees
c) No standard, since the accounts are closed
= a
why: Excluding terminated accounts makes performance incomplete and misleading.

? A member's country has no law on gifts, and her firm allows any gift. A covered company offers her a luxury holiday. She should:
a) accept, since law and firm policy allow it
b) decline, because I(B) applies regardless of local rules
c) accept and disclose it in her next report
= b
why: The Code is stricter than the absent law. A lavish gift from a covered company could compromise independence, so she should decline.
